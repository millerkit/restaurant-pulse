What the covenant requires, in plain terms

Our Eastern Bank loan has a rule we have to pass once a year: for every $1 we pay on our loans, the restaurant has to earn at least $1.25 before loan interest is paid. This is the bank’s way of checking that we earn comfortably more than we owe, with a 25% cushion.
For 2026 the loans counted (the SBA loan plus five investor notes) cost $89,136 in payments, so we need $111,420 of earnings before interest and depreciation (1.25 × $89,136). Loan interest ($80,015) and the $300K Section 179 depreciation (non-cash) are both added back, so even a projected loss of $250,688 works out to $129,327 of covenant earnings. We pass with about $17,907 to spare (1.45x). The catch: that projection counts a $88.1K gain from selling the old location for $100K (equipment, the lease and the liquor license). Those are still carried at $11.9K on the books after catch-up depreciation, so the gain is $100K less that. If the sale slips into 2027, or the bank won’t count a one-time gain as earnings, we’d be at 0.46x, about $70,143 short. Page 2 looks at that risk.

The risk: the gain on the asset sale

What happens if it doesn’t count for 2026, and how much extra covers in specific spaces can make up
On the current plan we pass the covenant with about $17.9K to spare. But $88.1K of the year’s earnings is the gain on the asset sale. The lease and liquor license transfers need approval from the landlord, the City and the state, so closing could slip into 2027. Without the sale we’d be at 0.46x and need about $70.1K more. October is landing on plan so far (Chef’s 11.9 a night against 12 planned, Dining 47.9 against 48, Bar 17 against 17), so any extra has to come from covers above the plan in November and December: about 48 service nights (23 in Nov, 25 in Dec).

The bottom line

Covers can’t replace the asset sale this year. Even running close to full every night in November and December makes up about 93% of the $70.1K. So the most important steps are closing the sale in 2026 and confirming with Eastern Bank that a one-time gain counts toward the covenant.
Extra covers still build cushion, and Chef’s Counter is the strongest lever: each extra Chef’s cover adds about $121 to earnings, against $36 in the Dining Room and $16 at the Bar.

Why covers, not prices

Higher spend does help: $5 more per Dining cover is worth about $7.9K. But that means raising prices or getting every table to order more, which is hard to do quickly. Bringing more people in is the bigger and more controllable lever. The likeliest spend gains are drinks: wine pairings at Chef’s and a second glass in the Dining Room.

Looking ahead: the covenant is tested every year. Over a full year, 2 more covers a night at Chef’s Counter is worth about $70K, about as much as the asset sale is adding this year, which makes it the lever for 2027.

  • Other income: the projection includes $288,441 of other income, $24.0K of it grant money and the $88.1K gain on the asset sale not yet received. Every dollar of it that slips comes straight off the cushion. The $300K depreciation figure is an estimate; Section 179 is limited to business income for the year, so the allowed amount may be lower. Depreciation does not change the covenant ratio, because it is added back.
